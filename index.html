About the data

What's counted, how it's measured, and where it comes from.

What's counted

All U.S. trade in catfish, meaning every species in the order Siluriformes. By U.S. law, only North American catfish (channel, blue and their relatives) can be sold as "catfish" in the United States, but trade codes count all catfish species, including Pangasius (basa, tra, swai), so this page does too. Use the Species filter on the Imports tab to see Ictalurus alone.

From 2012 on, the trade codes (Harmonized System) that hold only catfish:

Mixed codes that also hold other fish (such as tilapia, carp or eels) are left out, because they can't be split.

2005 to 2011

These years come from NOAA Fisheries' Foreign Trade database, which carries the Census Bureau's own trade figures under the catfish codes in use before 2012 (they match Census's own figures where both exist). Imports cover the same species and products as later years, apart from a small amount of whole fish of other catfish species. Exports before 2012 count fillets only; whole and dressed catfish exports are counted from 2012, so export totals step up that year.

Why it starts in 2005

Catfish imports were first reported by species in July 2004. Before that, much of the basa and tra imported from Vietnam was recorded as "marine fish" or "freshwater fish" fillets, mixed with other fish, so earlier catfish import figures would undercount it. 2005 is the first full year with complete catfish detail.

Species

Import records name the species: Ictalurus (channel and blue catfish), Pangasius (basa, tra and swai), or other catfish (Clarias, Silurus, and records that don't name a species). Export records don't name a species.

Fresh and frozen exports

Frozen fillets (0304.62) count as fresh exports on this page, with fresh fillets and fresh whole fish; only frozen whole or dressed fish (0303.24) counts as frozen. That follows NOAA Fisheries' product names, so these figures match NOAA's. To see the four products separately, use the Product filter on the Exports tab. For 2005 to 2011, exports are fillets only, and frozen fillets count as frozen.

How it's measured

  • Imports. Pounds count fish that cleared customs for use in the United States (Census calls these imports for consumption). Dollars are the customs value of all arrivals (general imports). NOAA Fisheries reports catfish trade the same way. A few shipments show a value but no pounds; they count in dollars only.
  • Catfish-only codes. Only product codes that hold catfish alone are counted. NOAA Fisheries' catfish totals also take in codes catfish shares with carp, eels, tilapia and other fish (mostly dried, salted or smoked fillets), so NOAA's import totals run about 1% higher than this page's.
  • Exports are domestic exports only: catfish of U.S. origin. Re-exports of imported catfish are left out.
  • Pounds are product weight, as shipped, converted from Census's kilograms (1 kg = 2.20462 lb). A pound of fillets is a pound of fillets, not the live weight of the fish it came from.
  • Dollars for imports are customs value: the price paid for the fish, not counting freight and insurance to the United States or duties (including antidumping and countervailing duties). For exports, dollars are the value at the U.S. port (free alongside ship), including inland freight but not ocean shipping. Price per pound is dollars divided by pounds. It is an average of what shipments were declared at, not a market price. Prices based on under 10,000 lb are marked few lb; price charts leave out years under 1,000 lb.
  • Inflation. Dollars are shown as reported unless you switch to adjusted dollars on the Imports and Exports tabs. Adjusted dollars are converted to the newest month's dollars with the Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U, U.S. city average, U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics). BLS published no CPI for October 2025 because of the government shutdown; that month uses the midpoint of September and November 2025.
  • Duties are not shown. Census's duty figure leaves out antidumping and countervailing duties, so it understates what importers pay.

When it updates

The U.S. Census Bureau releases each month's trade about five weeks after the month ends, at 8:30 a.m. Eastern on dates it publishes about a year ahead. This page updates the morning of each release. Each update also re-pulls the current and previous year, so recent months pick up Census's revisions.
